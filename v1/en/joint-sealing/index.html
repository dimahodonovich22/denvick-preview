The weakest point of any structure

Joints are everywhere: between a window and the facade, between two concrete slabs, between tiles in a swimming pool. They have to do two contradictory things at once: stay sealed and still move. A joint that is too stiff cracks. A joint that bonds poorly comes loose. Either way, water gets in, bringing moisture, dirt and damage with it. A good joint is therefore not a detail, but a prerequisite for a dry and durable structure.

Three types of joints

Silicone joints are used for sealing around windows and doors and in sanitary areas. They stay elastic, adhere to many materials and resist weather and UV.

Epoxy joints are hard, watertight and chemical resistant. They are ideal for swimming pools, containment bunds, kitchens and tiled floors that are cleaned intensively.

Expansion joints absorb the movement of large concrete slabs and structures. We work with elastic sealants, joint tapes and expansion profiles that withstand traffic.

Dimensions and product determine everything

How wide and deep a joint must be depends on the movement it has to absorb. With a backer rod, we make sure the sealant only adheres to the joint sides and can stretch freely. We choose the primer and the product according to the substrate and the load. Get that right and the joint lasts for years. Skip it and you will be resealing after one winter.

Joints in floors with traffic

In car parks, halls and on loading docks, expansion joints take a heavy beating. The wheels of cars and forklifts break up the edges and the joint itself gets damaged. There, we first repair the joint edges and, where needed, fit a robust joint profile. That way, the joint stays sealed and traffic passes over it smoothly.

Our approach

How we work

A joint must move without cracking and stay sealed without coming loose. That starts with the right dimensions and the right product.

  1. 01

    Assessment

    We look at the type of joint, the movement it must absorb and the exposure to water, traffic or chemicals.

  2. 02

    Removing the old joint

    We remove old sealant, jointing material and loose edges completely, so that the new joint goes onto a clean substrate.

  3. 03

    Repairing the edges

    We first rebuild damaged joint edges in concrete with a strong repair mortar.

    Concrete repair
  4. 04

    Backer rod and primer

    A backer rod sets the correct depth of the joint. A primer ensures adhesion to the joint sides.

  5. 05

    Sealing or fitting a profile

    Silicone, polyurethane, epoxy or a joint profile, depending on the movement and the load.

  6. 06

    Finishing

    We finish the joint neatly, so that it looks tidy and is easy to clean.

Frequently asked questions

Questions about joint sealing

Your question is not listed? Ask us directly.

What types of joints do you install?

Silicone joints for sealing around windows and doors, epoxy joints for swimming pools, containment bunds and tiles, and expansion joints with joint tapes and expansion profiles in floors and structures.

What is an expansion joint?

Concrete expands and shrinks with temperature changes. An expansion joint gives that movement room, so that the concrete does not crack. The joint must stay sealed while still being able to move.

Why does sealant come loose?

Usually because the joint is too deep or too shallow, or because the sealant adheres on three sides and therefore cannot move. With a backer rod and the right dimensions, we prevent that.

How long does a joint last?

That depends on the product, the load and the workmanship. A properly sized joint with the right product lasts many years. Joints in heavily loaded floors need checking from time to time.

Can damaged joint edges be repaired?

Yes. We rebuild crumbled edges with a rapid hardening repair mortar and, where needed, fit a joint profile that protects the edges against traffic.

How much does joint sealing cost?

That depends on the length and type of joint, the product and the accessibility. After a site visit, you will receive a clear, free quote with no obligation.
